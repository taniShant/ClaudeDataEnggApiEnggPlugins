# Vulnerable Customer Standards

> Source: FCA Finalised Guidance FG21/1, "Guidance for Firms on the Fair Treatment of Vulnerable Customers," February 2021.
> Legal basis: FCA Principle 6 (customers' interests), also Principles 2, 3, 7, and 9.
> Also read alongside `barclays-buk-standards:consumer-duty` — Consumer Duty Principle 12 builds on this foundation.

---

## Section 1: Core Definition and Principle

**Definition (FCA FG21/1, para 1.1):**
> "A vulnerable customer is someone who, due to their personal circumstances, is especially susceptible to harm, particularly when a firm is not acting with appropriate levels of care."

**Key clarifications:**
- Vulnerability is a **spectrum of risk**, not a binary state. All customers are at risk of becoming vulnerable.
- Vulnerability can be **temporary** (e.g. hospital stay), **long-term** (e.g. chronic illness), or **fluctuating**. It can increase or decrease over time.
- Characteristics are often **complex and overlapping** (e.g. bereavement → mental health impact → low resilience).
- The **label "vulnerable" must not be applied** to customers in direct interactions — focus on the harm or disadvantage they may be susceptible to, not the label.
- **Scale:** In 2020, 46% of UK adults (24.1 million people) had characteristics of vulnerability. During Covid-19 (Oct 2020) this rose to 53%.

**The fundamental outcome standard (para 1.3):**
> "Vulnerable consumers must experience outcomes as good as those for other consumers and receive consistently fair treatment."

## Section 2: The Four Drivers of Vulnerability

Every product design, journey design, and communications review must consider which of these drivers may be present in the target customer population.

### Health
- Severe or long-term illness
- Physical disability
- Hearing or visual impairment
- Mental health condition or disability
- Addiction
- Low mental capacity or cognitive disability

### Life Events
- Bereavement
- Retirement
- Income shock
- Relationship breakdown
- Domestic abuse (including economic control)
- Caring responsibilities
- Leaving care, migration, seeking asylum, human trafficking, modern slavery, convictions

### Resilience
- Over-indebtedness
- Inadequate or erratic income
- Low savings
- Low emotional resilience

### Capability
- Poor literacy or numeracy skills
- Poor English language skills
- Poor or non-existent digital skills
- Low knowledge or confidence in managing finances
- Learning difficulties
- No or low access to help or support

## Section 3: What Firms Must Do

### Four overarching obligations (para 1.9):

1. **Understand** the needs of the target market and customer base — including what vulnerability characteristics are likely present
2. Ensure staff have the **right skills and capability** to recognise and respond to vulnerability
3. **Respond** to customer needs through product design, flexible customer service, and accessible communications
4. **Monitor and assess** whether the needs of vulnerable customers are being met, and make improvements where not

### Fair treatment must be embedded — not just at frontline (para 1.7):
- In product development, not only in customer service
- Senior leaders must create and maintain a culture that enables fair treatment
- Policies and processes must embed fair treatment throughout the **whole customer journey**

## Section 4: Product and Journey Design Obligations

### Consider potential harms at every design stage:
- Idea generation
- Development and testing
- Launch
- Ongoing review

**Questions every product manager and UX designer must apply:**

1. What types of harm or disadvantage are customers in my target market vulnerable to?
2. Does this product or service have features that could exploit or harm vulnerable consumers, even unintentionally?
3. Does this journey allow a consumer to pause, exit, or get human support if they need it?
4. Is there a way for a customer to disclose their needs in this journey — including in digital-only channels?
5. Will a customer have to repeat their circumstances across touchpoints?
6. Does the design avoid rush — do consumers have sufficient time to understand and decide?
7. If we close or change this channel or feature, which vulnerable customers will be harmed?
8. Have vulnerable consumers been involved in testing this product or service?

### Inclusive design (para 4.7–4.10):
Design features that meet vulnerable customers' needs **while benefitting the wider market**. Examples:
- Plain English materials — benefits those with low literacy, cognitive disabilities, and the general population
- Online/text functions to notify firms of changed circumstances — allows privacy, reduces anxiety
- Optional payment blocks (e.g. gambling)
- Designated second contact / trusted person from onboarding
- Text boxes or chatbots in digital journeys to allow customers to share information about their needs

### Automated processes must have manual overrides (para 4.8):
Standard services with no manual override are harmful for consumers with cognitive disabilities. Any automated process that makes a decision materially affecting the customer must have an exception/escalation path.

### Channel closures require vulnerability impact assessment (para 4.22):
When closing channels (telephone banking, paper statements, branches), firms must:
- Understand the impact on vulnerable groups
- Communicate changes clearly with alternative solutions
- Ensure no vulnerable customer is effectively excluded

## Section 5: Customer Service Design

- Staff must be able to **"stop the clock"** — not penalised by volume/speed metrics when a vulnerable customer needs more time
- Pay and reward structures must reward **quality of service and outcomes**, not just volumes
- Automated helplines and digital journeys must include **exit lanes** to human support
- Firms must support **third-party access** — short-term mandates, Power of Attorney, warm referral to debt advisors, informal nominee arrangements
- Customers should **not have to repeat their circumstances** across touchpoints or teams ("tell us once" principle)
- Vulnerability information recorded on a customer's file must be **accessible to any employee** subsequently serving that customer

## Section 6: Identifying Vulnerability

### Staff must be trained to recognise vulnerability in three scenarios:
1. Where the consumer has **told** the firm about a need
2. Where there are **clear indicators** of vulnerability
3. Where there is **relevant information already on file**

### Proactive signs to watch for (para 3.14):
- Changes in payment behaviour: missed payments, unarranged overdrafts, sudden account inactivity
- Phrases: "I can't pay," "I'm having trouble," "I can't read my bill," "I can't understand the letter," mentions of debt advisor or breathing space
- Observable signs: confusion, asking for repetition, signs of distress, agitation

### In digital journeys:
- Include **text boxes, chat options, or explicit prompts** allowing customers to share information about their needs
- Do not design flows that require customers to navigate at speed — allow time and offer ways to pause

### The TEXAS model — structured tool for recording vulnerability (para 3.18):
- **T**hank the customer for their disclosure
- **E**xplain how their disclosed information will be used
- e**X**plicit consent sought (or check whether the customer objects)
- **A**sk questions to understand the situation better
- **S**ignpost to internal support or external services

## Section 7: Communications Requirements

- All communications — marketing, point of sale, post-contractual, change notifications, complaints — must be **clear and understandable** for vulnerable consumers
- Where standard communications don't meet needs, **alternative formats must be offered** where proportionate:
  - Large print
  - Audio
  - British/Irish Sign Language via video
  - Easy-read infographics
  - Braille
  - Accessible colour schemes (e.g. for dyslexia)
  - Accessible websites
  - Next Generation Text
- Firms must offer **multiple communication channels** — phone, email, in-branch, text, web chat, secure message, video
- Single-channel digital firms must actively consider whether they can serve vulnerable consumers and provide at least one additional channel
- Customers must **never feel rushed into decisions** — pop-ups, FAQs, and chatbots should help explain complex terms
- Firms must **proactively check understanding** for products likely to be purchased by vulnerable consumers — do not assume disclosure was understood

## Section 8: Data and Privacy Considerations

- Information about a customer's vulnerability circumstances may constitute **Special Category Data** under GDPR (Article 9) — particularly health-related information
- **Legitimate Interests three-part test** may be used to justify processing without explicit consent:
  1. Purpose test — is there a legitimate interest?
  2. Necessity test — is processing necessary for that purpose?
  3. Balancing test — does the legitimate interest override the individual's rights?
- Data must be **minimised** — collect only what is needed to provide appropriate support
- Systems must allow vulnerability flags to persist and be surfaced across customer touchpoints

## Section 9: Monitoring and Governance

### Firms must monitor (para 5.2–5.12):
- Outcomes vulnerable customers experience **compared to other customers**
- Monitoring is **continuous** — not a one-off exercise
- Complaints involving vulnerable customers vs. other customers (volume and root causes)
- Drop-off and completion rates across digital journeys — unexplained gaps may indicate accessibility or comprehension issues
- Contact rates with vulnerability support teams
- Training and competence records

### Management information to collect:
- Business persistence (cancellation rates and reasons)
- Legacy product distribution and pricing — are vulnerable customers paying more?
- Behavioural insights from digital channel use
- Customer feedback including social media and complaints
- File reviews and call monitoring

### Governance:
- Senior leaders are responsible for championing vulnerability culture
- Transformation programmes should have a **lead representing the interests of vulnerable customers**
- Firms must be able to demonstrate their approach during routine supervisory interactions, not just dedicated vulnerability exercises
- Fair treatment of vulnerable customers must be embedded in **product design governance**, not only in customer service training

## Section 10: Agent Self-Check — Before Finalising Any Output

When producing product requirements, UX designs, or communications, confirm:

- [ ] Target market vulnerability profile identified — which of the 4 drivers are likely present?
- [ ] Journey includes a way for customers to disclose needs (text box, chat, explicit prompt)
- [ ] Automated decision steps have a human escalation/exception path
- [ ] Journey allows pausing, exiting, or requesting human support
- [ ] Customer will not need to repeat their circumstances across touchpoints
- [ ] Communications offer alternative formats where relevant
- [ ] Multiple contact channels available (not digital-only without exception process)
- [ ] Plain language used — tested against 9-year-old reading age baseline
- [ ] No rush mechanisms — customers have time to understand before acting
- [ ] If a channel is being closed or changed — impact on vulnerable groups assessed
- [ ] Inclusive design considered — does this feature benefit the wider market as well as vulnerable customers?
